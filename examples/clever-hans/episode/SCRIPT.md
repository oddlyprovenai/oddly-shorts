# 02 — The Horse That Could Count (SPIKE)

## Facts and sources (Wikipedia, "Clever Hans", API extract read 2026-11-16; O. Pfungst, Clever Hans, 1907/1911)
- Hans, a horse owned by Wilhelm von Osten, "a gymnasium mathematics teacher", appeared to do arithmetic in Germany in the early 20th century; taught to "add, subtract, multiply, divide, work with fractions, tell the time, keep track of the calendar".
- "If the eighth day of the month comes on a Tuesday, what is the date of the following Friday? Hans would answer by tapping his hoof eleven times."
- "Von Osten exhibited Hans throughout Germany and never charged admission."
- "Carl Stumpf formed a panel of 13 people, known as the Hans Commission"; Pfungst found the horse answered correctly even when von Osten did not ask, "ruling out the possibility of fraud".
- "the horse gave the right answer only when the questioner knew what the answer was and the horse could see the questioner."
- Pfungst 1907: Hans responded to involuntary cues in the questioner's body language; this is why experiments are made double-blind.

## Narration
A horse in Germany could add, subtract and tell the time. Or so everyone thought. Germany, the early 1900s. His name was Clever Hans. His owner, a maths teacher, asked the questions, and Hans tapped the answers with his hoof. Fractions. Dates. The calendar. If the eighth is a Tuesday, what date is Friday? Hans tapped eleven times. Correct. Crowds came to watch, and he never charged a penny. A commission of thirteen experts came to check, and found no cheating. Then a psychologist, Oskar Pfungst, tried one thing. He asked questions he didn't know the answer to. Hans got them wrong. And when Hans couldn't see the person asking, he failed too. Luckily, Hans wasn't lying. Unluckily, he wasn't counting either. He was reading faces: the tiny nod when he reached the right number. It's like a quiz where the host smiles just before your answer. That's why careful tests are double-blind today: nobody in the room knows the answer. And Hans? Still clever. Just not at maths. So we test our router on 130 requests it has never seen, written blind. It got 116 right. Oddly Proven.
