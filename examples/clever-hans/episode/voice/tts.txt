A horse in Germany could add, subtract and tell the time. Or so everyone thought. Germany, the early nineteen hundreds. His name was Clever Hans. His owner, a maths teacher, asked the questions, and Hans tapped the answers with his hoof. Fractions. Dates. The calendar. If the eighth is a Tuesday, what date is Friday? Hans tapped eleven times. Correct. Crowds came to watch, and he never charged a penny. A commission of thirteen experts came to check, and found no cheating. Then a psychologist, Oskar Pfungst, tried one thing. He asked questions he didn't know the answer to. Hans got them wrong. And when Hans couldn't see the person asking, he failed too. Luckily, Hans wasn't lying. Unluckily, he wasn't counting either. He was reading faces: the tiny nod when he reached the right number. It's like a quiz where the host smiles just before your answer. That's why careful tests are double-blind today: nobody in the room knows the answer. And Hans? Still clever. Just not at maths. So we test our router on a hundred and thirty requests it has never seen, written blind. It got a hundred and sixteen right. Oddly Proven.
